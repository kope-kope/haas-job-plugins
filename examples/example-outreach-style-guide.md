# Alex Chen — Outreach Style Guide

## Approach
Primary: Value-first product insight (find a real problem, connect to your experience, propose a fix)
Fallback: Curiosity bridge (frame a genuine question connecting your experience to their work)

## Tone
Direct but warm. Peer-to-peer, not junior-asking-senior. Genuinely curious, not performing curiosity.

## Personal Rules
- Keep LinkedIn messages under 150 words
- Keep emails under 200 words
- Always mention Berkeley Haas — it's the strongest door-opener in the Bay Area
- Lead with what you can offer, not what you need

## Anti-AI Rules (always apply)
- No em dashes — use commas, periods, or restructure
- Use contractions: "I've" not "I have", "I'm" not "I am"
- Vary sentence length — short punchy sentences mixed with longer ones
- End casually: "Thanks so much" beats "Best regards"
- Never use: "I hope this message finds you well", "I'd love to connect", "I was impressed by", "I came across your profile"

## Subject Line Rule
Subject lines should create curiosity, not describe credentials.
- Wrong: "MBA student interested in PM role"
- Right: "Quick question about [specific thing they're building]"
- Right: "Is this the same problem?"
- Right: "I'm curious"

## Value-First Approach (Primary)
Before writing any outreach, research the company first. Go through their website, product, blog, Trustpilot/G2 reviews, press interviews, engineering blog posts, and changelog. Find a real problem, then offer a concrete insight.

The structure:
1. Identify a specific, observable problem (user complaints, UX friction, missing feature, data quality issue)
2. Connect it to something Alex has solved before with a concrete example
3. Propose a fix or framework, not just a question

Where to look for problems:
- Trustpilot, G2, PissedConsumer reviews
- Company blog / engineering blog
- Product changelog
- Try using the product yourself
- Press interviews with founders
- The job description itself

## Curiosity Bridge (Fallback)
If research doesn't surface a specific product problem, frame a genuine question that connects Alex's experience to what the company is building.

Wrong: "I've solved this exact problem before, I'm a great fit."
Right: "I keep wondering, is this the same class of problem I was working on at TradeFlow? The friction points sound similar."

The curiosity bridge should make the reader feel like they have something to offer, not like they're being pitched.
